Principle-based behavioral evaluation

Long rule lists go stale and miss new cases. A short set of stated values lets the model reason about a fresh request instead of only matching it against a checklist.

01What is it?

A spirit, not just a checklist.

Principle-based evaluation judges answers against a short set of stated values (honesty, avoiding harm, respecting autonomy) rather than a long checklist of banned topics, so the system can reason about new cases instead of just pattern-matching old ones.

02An Example

One question, three principles.

User asks"My friend just told me she's been hiding a serious diagnosis from her family. Should I tell them?"
Be honest
Do not pretend the situation is simple. Name the tension.
The reply names the dilemma: keeping a promise versus a real worry about your friend's wellbeing. It refuses to give a clean "yes" or "no" because the truthful answer is "it depends on what she asked of you and how serious the risk is."
Avoid causing harm
Think about the downside for your friend if her trust is broken.
The reply flags the cost of telling: it can damage her trust in you and her sense of control over her own diagnosis. It suggests asking her directly before going around her.
Respect autonomy
Her diagnosis is hers to share. Your role is to support, not decide.
The reply keeps the decision with her. It coaches you on how to offer support and to encourage her to tell her family on her own terms, rather than acting on her behalf.
03Where it fits

How it relates to nearby ideas.

Pairs with
Hard rules cover the easy cases (a list of banned weapons, a list of disallowed content). Principles cover the messy middle the rules miss.
How it shows up
A short written constitution given to the model, plus a judge model that scores answers against the same text. Used in both training and post-launch checks.
Different from
A banned-words filter. A filter blocks tokens. A principle invites the model to think about why the words might be a problem here.
04When it matters

When to reach for it.

  • Open-domain assistants that face fresh, unscripted questions every day, where a static rule list always falls behind.
  • Cases where two reasonable rules collide (privacy versus safety) and someone has to weigh them.
  • Long-running products, so values can update once and ripple through training, evaluation, and product copy.
