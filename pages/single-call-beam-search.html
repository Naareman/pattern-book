Beam Search

Keep the top-N candidate sequences alive simultaneously during decoding, then return the one with the highest overall score.

01What It Is

Race N beams to the finish.

Instead of committing to one word at each step, beam search keeps the best few half-finished answers alive at once and only commits at the end, to the one with the best overall score. The demo below shows it in action. The cost: keeping N answers alive means roughly N times the work.

02An Example

Three beams, two steps.

Beam width = 3  |  Translating: "Je suis content"
Step 1
"I am"
log-p: -0.41
"I'm"
log-p: -0.58
"We are"
log-p: -1.12
Step 2
"I am happy"
log-p: -0.62 ✓
"I'm happy"
log-p: -0.79
"I am glad"
log-p: -1.04
"We are happy"
log-p: -1.33
At step 2 only the top 3 beams survive. "I am happy" wins on cumulative log-probability. A greedy decoder would have committed to "I am" and never considered "I'm".
03Where It Fits

Where it fits.

Home
A way of shaping the output as the model writes it. The search runs inside one call to one model and decides which wording comes back.
Neighbor
Self-Consistency also produces several answers from one model and picks a winner. The difference: beam search tracks an overall score; self-consistency samples at random and takes the most common answer.
Not this
Once different models or different prompts generate the candidates and the results are merged, you have left a single call and moved to a different layer (combining outputs).
In short: several candidate wordings, all inside one call to one model, with the search happening as the answer is written.
04When It Matters

When to reach for it.

  • Best for short, structured outputs where getting the whole phrase right matters: translation, speech-to-text, math expressions.
  • Skip it for long or creative writing: it tends to produce repetitive, "safe" text, where sampling with some randomness reads better.
  • The extra compute means it is rarely the default on big hosted models, but it is common in open-model serving tools.
