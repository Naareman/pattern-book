Streaming cost attribution

When a stream is cut, decide what counts: only what the user kept, all of what was generated, or something in between.

01What is it?

A bill that matches the reality.

Streaming pricing has to deal with a question that batch pricing avoids: when the user cancels at word 80 of 400, what do you charge? The token cost upstream is real (the model produced something) and the user value downstream may be tiny (they did not even read it). The policy is a product decision, not just an accounting one: charge for tokens generated, charge for tokens delivered, refund on safety stops, never charge when the user cuts the first second. The choice shapes user trust, agent runs (which can rack up a bill quickly), and your own dashboards. Whatever the policy, the same partial-counter has to be measured at cut time and recorded with the rest of the request.

02An example

Same cut, three policies.

Reply cut at word 80 of an expected 400
Policy: charge for tokens generated. The model produced 105 tokens before the cancel arrived. Even though only 80 reached the user, all 105 are billable because the compute already happened.

03Where it fits

How it sits next to nearby ideas.

What it does
Maps a partial stream to a bill. Picks which counter (generated, delivered, accepted) becomes the price.
Pairs with
Pairs tightly with clean stopping: the cut writes the exact counters that the bill needs. Pairs with safety: a refund rule may apply when a guard stops the stream.
Not this
It is a product and pricing decision, not a metering technology. The counters exist regardless; the question is which ones become money.
04When it matters

When to reach for it.

  • Pay-as-you-go products where the user sees a token meter or a per-message price.
  • Agent platforms where a single run can cancel many times and bills can grow quickly.
  • Anywhere a safety stop, a quality cut, or an error must come with a clear bill story.
