Causal analysis

Causal analysis keeps asking "why" past the first explanation, so the fix lands at the source instead of the surface.

01What It Is

The first answer is rarely the cause.

When something fails, the first thing you see is the symptom: a wrong number, a broken tool call, a stale citation. The first explanation that lands is usually a proximate cause: the closest piece in the chain. Fixing only that piece patches today's failure and lets tomorrow's slip through under a new disguise. Causal analysis keeps walking back: each answer becomes the next question. The technique most teams use is the five whys: keep asking "why" until you reach something a single owner can actually change. That last "why" is the root cause.

02An Example

Tap "ask why" to go one level deeper.

Symptom: the bot quoted last year's refund policy to a customer this week
0Symptom
What did the user see?
The bot returned the 2025 refund window (14 days) instead of the current one (30 days).
1Why
Why did it answer with the old window?
The retrieval step pulled in the 2025 policy document instead of the 2026 one.
2Why
Why did it pull the older document?
Both versions live in the index. The 2025 one ranks higher because it has been seen more often.
3Why
Why is the old one still in the index?
The reindex job runs nightly, but the deprecation flag on old docs is checked only on full rebuild.
4Why
Why is the deprecation flag ignored on the nightly job?
The flag was added last quarter and never wired into the nightly ingestion path. Only the full rebuild path knows about it.
5Why
Why was the new path missed?
There is no shared filter step between the two ingestion paths. Each path implements its own filtering from scratch.
Root cause: the two ingestion paths do not share a filter step. The fix is to extract one shared filter and call it from both paths, then add a test that fails if either path skips it. Patching the nightly job alone would leave the same gap waiting in any future path.
step 0 of 5
If you stop at step 1, you fix tomorrow's broken bot. If you stop at step 5, the next bot built on the same ingestion does not break the same way.
03Where It Fits

How it relates to nearby ideas.

What it is
A way of thinking about a failure, not a tool. The discipline is to keep going past the first plausible answer.
Sits next to
Pairs with tracing (the evidence), replay (the test bench), drift detection (the trigger), and incident postmortems (the place a root cause gets written down so the team learns).
04When It Matters

When to reach for it.

  • The same kind of failure keeps coming back even after the obvious fix has shipped.
  • A failure touches more than one piece of the system and you suspect the cause sits earlier than where it showed up.
  • You are writing a postmortem and need the lasting fix, not just the patch that quieted the alert.

Sources
  • Ohno, T. (1988). Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production. Productivity Press. The original framing of the five whys as a habit for finding the cause behind the cause.
  • Allspaw, J. (2012). Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture. Etsy Engineering blog. Argues that the question "why" is only useful in a culture that treats people as part of a system, not the cause of the bug.
  • Rasmussen, J. (1997). Risk management in a dynamic society: a modelling problem. Safety Science, 27(2/3). A foundational paper on tracing failures back through nested system layers instead of stopping at the operator.
