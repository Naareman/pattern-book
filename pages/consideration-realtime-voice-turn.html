Voice turn-taking and interrupting

In a voice call, two people share one channel. The system has to know whose turn it is, and stop talking the moment the user starts.

01What is it?

Spoken turns are negotiated live.

Text chat has a clear handoff: I send, you send. Voice does not. A voice assistant has to listen and speak at the same time, and decide in milliseconds when its turn is up. Turn-taking is the moment-by-moment dance of figuring out who holds the floor: detecting when the user has stopped talking, detecting when they have started talking again, and choosing whether their new audio is a question, a quick yes, or an interruption. Interruption handling (sometimes called barge-in) is the action that follows: cut the spoken reply, drop the audio that has not been played yet, save what was said so far, and start listening properly. Done well, it feels like a normal conversation. Done badly, the bot talks over the user or stays silent for an awkward beat.

03Where it fits

How it sits next to nearby ideas.

What it does
Decides whose turn it is on a shared voice channel and acts on a barge-in: cut the audio, drop the unread tail, switch to listening.
Pairs with
Sits on top of clean stopping (the cut sequence) and first-word time (so the next reply starts fast). Voice activity detection feeds the turn decision.
Not this
It is not just speech-to-text. The transcript is one input; deciding when the user has the floor is a separate, ongoing call.
04When it matters

When to reach for it.

  • Any voice product where the user expects to talk over the assistant the way they would a person.
  • Customer support, accessibility, and hands-free use, where a wrong-turn moment frustrates fast.
  • Phone calls and headsets, where audio cannot be unsaid once it plays.
