Modality-specific evaluation

When the right answer to a visual or spoken question can be phrased many different ways, simple text matching breaks. Scoring needs to ask about meaning, not wording.

01What Is It?

Score the meaning, not the words.

Modality-specific evaluation replaces simple text matching with a fact rubric scored by a judge model that reads both the answer and the original image or audio, because visual or spoken questions often have dozens of equally valid wordings.

02An Example

"Describe this photo" scored two ways.

Gold answer: "A red car parked in front of a white house."
Model said:
A crimson sedan sits outside a pale-coloured home.
word overlap: 1 of 9 verdict: fail

The meaning is right, but the words barely overlap, so the score is wrong.

Rubric (what the answer must mention):
[ ] a car · [ ] colour red · [ ] a house · [ ] colour white · [ ] car is in front of house
Model said:
A crimson sedan sits outside a pale-coloured home.
car red house white in front of

A judge ticks four of five facts. Score: 4/5. Phrasing freedom is allowed, meaning is still checked.

03Where It Fits

How it sits next to nearby ideas.

A flavour of
The general "judge model" approach used for text scoring. Here the judge also reads the original image or audio, so it can check the answer against the source.
Pairs with
Grounding. Rubric items often double as grounding checks: each item asks "is this in the input?" instead of "is this in the gold string?"
04When It Matters

When to think about it.

  • Answers to your image or audio prompts vary in wording even when they mean the same thing.
  • Plain accuracy numbers look low because the model paraphrases the gold answer.
  • You want partial credit for partly-right answers, not just pass or fail.
