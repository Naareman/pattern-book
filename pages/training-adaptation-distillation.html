Teaching a small model from a big one

Run the big model on a pile of questions, keep its answers, and train a smaller model to copy them. The small one ends up far better than if it had learned alone.

01What is it?

A small model learns from a big model's worked answers.

The big "teacher" model is used to generate answers (and often the reasoning behind them) for a large batch of inputs; those question-and-answer pairs become the training material for a much smaller "student" model that runs cheaper and faster while keeping a good chunk of the teacher's quality.

02An example

A 70-billion teacher trains a 7-billion student.

10x smaller, most of the quality. The student gets to copy carefully worked answers it could never have produced on its own; the teacher is only needed once, during training.
03Where it fits

Where it sits in the training story.

It needs
A strong big model you trust and a small base model to teach. You also need permission to use the big model's answers as training data (check the license).
Not the same as
Just calling the big model at run time. Once the student is trained, you do not need the teacher any more: cheaper to run, easier to host yourself.
04When it matters

When to reach for it.

  • You need the model to run on cheaper hardware, a phone, or with stricter latency budgets.
  • You want to bake a big model's behaviour into something you can host yourself, with no run-time call to anyone else.
  • The big model is too costly to call for every user request at the volume you expect.

Sources
  • Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015). Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network. arXiv:1503.02531. The foundational paper that named the technique and explained why students learn faster from a teacher's answers than from raw labels.
  • Taori et al. (2023). Alpaca: A Strong, Replicable Instruction-Following Model. Stanford CRFM. A small open base model trained on instructions written by a much larger model; an early demonstration in the chat-model era.
  • Hsieh et al. (2023). Distilling Step-by-Step. arXiv:2305.02301. Shows that copying the teacher's working, not just its final answers, gives the smaller student a bigger lift.
